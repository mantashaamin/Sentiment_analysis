PHASE 1: DATASET EXPLORATION

1. OBJECTIVE

The objective of this phase was to understand the selected
Google Play Store reviews dataset before performing data
cleaning, NLP preprocessing and machine learning.

We explored the dataset structure, columns, missing values,
duplicate reviews, star ratings, application distribution,
sentiment distribution and review lengths.

The purpose was to understand the quality and characteristics
of our data before making further implementation decisions.


2. DATASET INFORMATION

Dataset: Google Play Store App Reviews

Dataset file:
dataset/raw/google_play_reviews.csv

Total records: 16,092
Total columns: 12
Total applications: 15
Unique review texts: 10,663

The dataset contains reviews from multiple productivity and
personal-organisation applications, including task management,
habit tracking, focus, planning and calendar applications.

Important columns:

- content: Actual review text.
- score: Star rating given by the user.
- appId: Application identifier.

Other columns include reviewId, userName, userImage,
thumbsUpCount, reviewCreatedVersion, at, replyContent,
repliedAt and sortOrder.

5. MISSING VALUE ANALYSIS

Observed results:

Column                  Missing Values

reviewCreatedVersion       2,350
replyContent               8,203
repliedAt                  8,203
All other columns              0

Important observation:

The main columns required for our project, content, score
and appId, contain no missing values.

Decision:

We will not remove reviews simply because optional metadata
such as developer replies or application version information
is missing.

Reason:

Our main objective is to analyse review text. Missing developer
replies or version information does not make the review text
unusable for sentiment classification.


6. DUPLICATE ANALYSIS

Observed results:

Total records:                 16,092
Completely duplicated rows:         0
Repeated review text occurrences: 5,429
Unique review texts:           10,663

Further investigation:

We grouped reviews by their exact text and checked whether
the same text had different star ratings.

Result:

64 unique review texts were found with conflicting ratings.

Examples:

Review: Amazing app
Ratings: 3, 4, 5

Review: Awesome
Ratings: 4, 5

Review: Best
Ratings: 1, 3

Review: Cool
Ratings: 4, 5

Why this matters:

Our sentiment labels are generated from star ratings.
Therefore, identical review texts with different ratings
can receive different sentiment labels.

Decision:

We will not blindly remove all repeated reviews.

We need to investigate conflicting review groups before
finalising duplicate handling.

We must also prevent identical review texts from appearing
in both training and testing data, wherever practical,
to avoid data leakage.

8. SENTIMENT LABEL CREATION

We proposed converting numerical star ratings into
three sentiment categories.

Mapping:

1-2 Stars = Negative
3 Stars   = Neutral
4-5 Stars = Positive

Derived sentiment distribution:

Sentiment       Reviews       Percentage

Negative         5,188          32.24%
Neutral          5,150          32.00%
Positive         5,754          35.76%

Total           16,092         100%

Observation:

The three sentiment classes are reasonably balanced.

This is useful because our classification model will not
face a severe overall class imbalance.

Important limitation:

These sentiment labels are derived from star ratings,
not independently annotated by human reviewers.

A three-star review may contain both positive and negative
opinions. Therefore, our model learns to predict
rating-derived sentiment labels.

We must clearly mention this limitation in our final report.

12. RESEARCH PAPER CONNECTION

The dataset exploration and sentiment-label decisions are
supported by ideas discussed in the following papers.

PAPER 1:

Pang, B. and Lee, L. (2005).

"Seeing Stars: Exploiting Class Relationships for Sentiment
Categorization with Respect to Rating Scales."

Research idea used:

The paper studies sentiment categorisation using rating
scales and highlights the relationship between ratings
and sentiment categories.

Our implementation:

We use star ratings to derive three sentiment classes:
Negative, Neutral and Positive.

We are not implementing the specialised algorithm
proposed in the paper.

PAPER 2:

Pang, B. and Lee, L. (2008).

"Opinion Mining and Sentiment Analysis."

Research idea used:

The paper provides an overview of sentiment analysis,
text classification, feature representation and challenges
in opinion mining.

Our implementation:

We use its general sentiment-analysis foundations to guide
our dataset understanding and subsequent NLP methodology.


13. IMPORTANT DECISIONS MADE

1. We selected Google Play Store reviews from 15
   productivity and personal-organisation applications.

2. We decided to perform three-class sentiment
   classification.

3. We will use the content column as our main text input.

4. We will use the score column to derive sentiment labels.

5. We will use appId for application-wise analysis.

6. We will retain the original dataset unchanged.

7. We will investigate repeated reviews instead of
   automatically deleting them.

8. We will prevent duplicate-text leakage during evaluation.

9. We will evaluate the model across different applications
   to investigate generalisation.

10. We will clearly acknowledge that sentiment labels
    are derived from star ratings.
